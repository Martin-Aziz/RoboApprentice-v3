01 · The thesis, up top

Own the task-intent layer between people and industrial robots.

Everyone else is fighting over the robot — the hardware, the model, the motion. The defensible company is the one that captures, validates, versions, approves, and remembers the task across whatever robot or model executes it. That layer is software, it's robot-agnostic by necessity, and nobody owns it yet.

One sentence

Aurevix is the deployment-and-trust layer for industrial automation: show it a task once, and it produces validated, auditable, redeployable robot work — across the robots a factory already owns — without an integrator on the critical path.

Three reasons this beats "no-code programming from video," which is a feature inside the above, not a company:

  1. Video-to-code is contestable; task memory + validation is not. Standard Bots, Wandelbots, Intrinsic and the VLA labs can all generate motion. None of them owns a permissioned, cross-brand record of why a task is the way it is, what it's allowed to do, how it was signed off, and how it has drifted. That record compounds.
  2. The market's blocker is trust, not motion. IFR's 2026 read is explicit: AI autonomy makes "testing, validation, and human oversight much more complex — but more necessary." The thing standing between a working demo and a deployed cell is a safety review and a liability question. That's a product, and you're already building it (the deploy-readiness gate).
  3. Robot-agnostic is the only durable surface — but as a task layer, not an OS. The universal-OS version of agnosticism died with ForgeOS because OEMs control the runtime. The task-intent version doesn't fight the OEM runtime; it sits above it and exports into it. Same agnosticism, different altitude, survivable.
The honest tension

This thesis is more defensible but slower to prove than "90% faster programming." It requires you to sell a system of record, which means landing the wedge (fast retasking) and then expanding into memory, validation, and approval before a competitor with more capital does the same. The race is real: Standard Bots explicitly frames deployments → data → better models as their flywheel. Your counter is that your flywheel spans the installed brownfield fleet, not one vendor's arm.

06 · Aurevix product strategy

Build the moat in sequence. Don't claim it early.

No moat exists yet — that's a feature of the stage, not a problem. The moat is constructed in a deliberate order, where each layer makes the next more valuable and harder to copy.

The moat-construction order

Layer 1 — Land

Operator-owned retasking on the installed base. The wedge that gets you in the door. Differentiate on "your existing UR/FANUC/KUKA/ABB robots," not the demo trick. Contested, so win on brownfield + validation.

Layer 2 — Gate

Deploy-readiness + safety sign-off. Turns a demo into a deployment and makes you the system buyers route approvals through. This is what competitors can't easily bolt on, and it rides the IFR liability trend.

Layer 3 — Remember

Task-intent record + versioning + audit log. The cornered resource. Every task ever taught, with its why/constraints/sign-off/drift. Creates switching cost and a cross-brand knowledge graph nobody else holds.

Layer 4 — Compound

Permissioned data flywheel + cross-site passport. Aggregate (with permission) what works across customers into better feasibility, better grasp suggestions, better readiness calibration. This is where the flywheel finally out-runs a single-vendor (Standard Bots) flywheel — because yours spans the fleet.

Why this order beats a better flywheel argument

Standard Bots will out-spend you on the model flywheel. You don't win the model race; you win the record race. A permissioned, cross-brand task-and-approval record is something a hardware company structurally can't build (their data is locked to their arm) and a model lab won't build (it's not their layer). Sequence the product so that by the time the record is deep, it's the reason customers can't leave.

07 · KineTrace strategy

A real wedge in a brutal neighborhood — keep it focused or keep it inside.

KineTrace's core insight is right: robotics data needs physics-aware annotation (micro-slips, torque anomalies, contact transitions, friction), not generic video labeling. The question isn't whether the gap is real — it's whether you fight for it as a standalone line against very well-funded labs, or use it as Aurevix's data engine.

The honest competitive read

Who you'd be competing with

The robot-data layer is where the frontier labs are spending hardest. Physical Intelligence is actively researching human-video→robot transfer and embodied memory; NVIDIA (Isaac / GR00T / Cosmos) is building the data + sim stack; and the OXE/RLDS ecosystem plus a wave of academic data-augmentation work (RDT2, OXE-AUGE, Galaxea G0) is moving monthly. A horizontal "Scale AI for robot data" sells into teams who increasingly build this in-house. That's why KineTrace's standalone score sits at Strong, not Exceptional — the gap is real, the buyer is sophisticated and partly self-supplying.

Where a defensible wedge actually exists

Sharpest

Failure mining as the entry point

Labs drown in success data and starve for labeled near-failures — slips, recoveries, unstable contacts, missed detections. "Find every near-failed grasp in this dataset and explain it" is a wedge that's painful, specific, and under-served. Lead here, not with general annotation.

Strong

Physics-event labels humans can't make

The differentiator vs. generic labeling: torque/contact/friction events from MCAP / ROS bag / HDF5 / RLDS + force-torque streams. This is genuinely hard and genuinely valuable — but the technical quality must be real, or sophisticated buyers will see through it instantly.

Strong

Dataset quality / leakage scoring

"Why did my policy fail?" → missing examples, bad sync, distribution shift, train/test leakage. A debugger for robot-learning data is a sticky, repeated-use tool that a one-off labeling vendor isn't.

Watch

The "GitHub/Datadog for robot data" framing

Evocative, but it's a platform ambition that needs a wedge first. Earn it through failure mining + physics events; don't lead with the platform metaphor.

The strategic call

The highest-leverage version of KineTrace may be internal: it's the annotation + failure-mining engine that turns Aurevix's permissioned flywheel into the moat in §06. Every deployed Aurevix task generates exactly the physics-event and failure data KineTrace is built to label — a data advantage no pure-software competitor and no single-hardware vendor can match. Run KineTrace as Aurevix's data engine first; spin it out as a standalone line only once it has a proprietary dataset (from Aurevix deployments) that the labs can't replicate. That sequencing turns a crowded standalone bet into a compounding asset.

Best standalone customers, if you do go external

In rough order of pain × budget × reachability: industrial-robot AI teams and warehouse-manipulation startups (concrete failure cost, reachable), then robot OEMs building learning stacks, then humanoid companies (huge budgets but heavy in-house builds), then academic labs (reachable, low budget — good for credibility, not revenue). The frontier foundation-model labs are the worst first customers: highest capability to build it themselves.

10 · Validate with design partners

Ten questions that decide the roadmap.

These are ordered to test the thesis itself — the task-layer bet lives or dies on the answers to 1–4. Take them to EMC, to Zachary at Neovia, and to the first integrator pilot.

  1. What does a single task change actually cost you today — in dollars, downtime, and wait-for-the-integrator days? (Sizes the wedge and validates the $/cycle outcome price.)
  2. What specifically makes you call an integrator vs. handle it in-house? (Defines the exact line your product has to cross to remove them from the critical path.)
  3. What must be true before your safety/EHS person signs off on a robot task? (Designs the sign-off workflow — your #3 gap — from the real gating criteria.)
  4. Who today owns "why is this program the way it is," and what breaks when they leave? (Tests whether the task-record moat solves a felt pain.)
  5. What would you trust an operator (not an engineer) to change unsupervised — and what never? (Calibrates the deploy-readiness thresholds against real risk tolerance.)
  6. How often do you actually retask, and is it predictable or reactive? (Validates HMLV frequency and the monitoring/drift value.)
  7. What has to pass in simulation before you'll run a new task on a real cell? (Defines what "feasibility" and "readiness" must actually check.)
  8. Which gripper/EOAT failures hurt most, and how do you catch them now? (Scopes the gripper-intelligence layer to real failure modes.)
  9. Would you pay monthly for retasking software — and does outcome-based (pay-per-successful-cycle) feel safer or scarier than a flat fee? (Validates the pricing model directly, including the cap.)
  10. If we made your existing UR/FANUC/KUKA robots retaskable without new hardware, what's the first task you'd put on it? (Tests the brownfield positioning and surfaces the highest-value first deployment.)
What you're really testing

If partners light up on 1–4 (cost of change, integrator trigger, sign-off criteria, ownership of intent), the task-layer thesis is validated and you should build the gate + record next. If they only light up on 5–6 and 10 (retasking frequency, brownfield), you have a strong wedge but need to earn the right to the moat through usage. Either answer is useful — but they point to different sequencing.

11 · Final thesis

The robot was never the hard part.

The robotics market is about to be flooded with capital, hardware, and foundation models that can all generate motion. Standard Bots will sell arms; Physical Intelligence and NVIDIA will sell brains; Wandelbots and Intrinsic will sell platforms. What none of them is building — because hardware companies can't and model labs won't — is the layer that turns a robot into a repeatable, trusted, auditable production worker across the robots a factory already owns.

The thesis

The winning industrial-AI software company will not generate motion. It will capture task intent, validate deployment readiness, manage safety sign-off, remember every task and its history, monitor execution for drift, and let factories retask their existing robots without depending on outside experts — and it will charge for the outcome, not the compute. Aurevix should own the task layer between human workers and industrial robots. The motion-generation demo is how you get in the room; the validated, remembered, signed-off task record is why you stay.

If you do one thing this quarter

Move the pitch up to where the product already is. Stop leading with "90% faster in 30 seconds" — a claim your best-funded competitor can now match — and start leading with the thing only you are building: "the deployment-and-trust layer that makes the robots you already own retaskable, validated, and signed off." Then build the gate and the record behind it, in that order, before someone with more money builds the same and calls it theirs first.

The discipline that wins

You set the right rules already: no premature moat claims, no unverified stats, credibility over magic, value-based pricing. The market in 2026 rewards exactly that posture — investors on the Standard Bots round paid a billion dollars for "actually usable on the floor," not for a flashier demo. Keep optimizing for truth. It's also, conveniently, the most defensible strategy you have.
