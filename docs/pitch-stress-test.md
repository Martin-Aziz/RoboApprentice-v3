## PRE-MORTEM (Phase 7)

### Scenario 1 — The design partnership never becomes production proof
**Probability:** High  
Eighteen months later, the investor says: “Aurevix had a beautiful demo and smart positioning, but the first design partners never converted into repeatable paid deployments. Every customer wanted a different robot, gripper, fixture, safety process, and integration path. The product worked in controlled demos but could not produce enough validated task records to prove reliability. The company became trapped between software margins and services-heavy deployment. By the time they narrowed the wedge, investor confidence was gone.”  
**Early indicator months 1–3:** Signed design partner still lacks paid status, narrow scope, deployment date, success criteria, or safety owner.  
**Hedge:** Put a hard design-partner success contract in the deck: one task, one robot, one deadline, one quantified baseline, one validation artifact.

### Scenario 2 — Liability and safety concerns slow every deal
**Probability:** High-medium  
“Aurevix underestimated how conservative industrial buyers are when software generates code for machines that can injure people. Legal, safety, and operations teams stretched pilots for months. The AI gating language helped demos, but buyers still asked who was liable when generated code failed. Without a certification roadmap, insurer comfort, and clear human-approval workflow, procurement treated Aurevix as unsafe experimental software.”  
**Early indicator months 1–3:** Every serious prospect asks for liability terms, safety documentation, or risk assessment before product scope.  
**Hedge:** Add a safety architecture slide: human verified, simulation-first, no autonomous deployment, audit log, customer safety process, liability boundaries, ISO roadmap.

### Scenario 3 — The product becomes a custom integrator in disguise
**Probability:** Medium  
“The team won early pilots by doing heavy manual work behind the scenes. Each customer required custom adapters, cell configuration, gripper logic, and debugging. Revenue looked real, but margins and deployment speed did not scale. Investors realized the software abstraction had not yet absorbed enough complexity to escape services.”  
**Early indicator months 1–3:** More than 30–40% of deployment time is bespoke engineering per customer.  
**Hedge:** Track adapter reuse, task-template reuse, deployment hours per cell, and percentage of workflow handled without custom code.

### Scenario 4 — Competitors collapse the wedge
**Probability:** Medium  
“Wandelbots, Vention, RoboDK, or an OEM shipped good-enough guided retasking for their installed base. Aurevix’s video-first UX was nice, but not enough to overcome distribution. Without exclusive task data, customer lock-in, or a channel, Aurevix became a feature rather than a company.”  
**Early indicator months 1–3:** Prospects say “our vendor has something similar” and ask for price comparison instead of workflow transformation.  
**Hedge:** Make Automation Passport and vendor-neutral memory the strategic center, not just video teaching.

### Scenario 5 — The data flywheel never activates
**Probability:** Medium-low  
“The pitch depended on task and validation data compounding, but customers refused cross-customer learning rights. Data remained siloed, sparse, and legally constrained. The model improved slowly, and the moat looked weaker each quarter.”  
**Early indicator months 1–3:** Design partners agree to use the product but reject training rights or data reuse.  
**Hedge:** Offer three data-rights tiers: isolated, anonymized aggregate learning, and paid data-partner program with explicit benefits.
